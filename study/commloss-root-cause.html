A site just went dark. Where is the break, how sure are we, and what should we do next?

Twelve two-week sprints that build one system while covering the ground two graduate courses will assume you already know. Probabilistic modeling first, reinforcement learning on top of it — because RL is decision-making layered over probabilistic models, and learning them in that order means the second half costs less.

What exists at week 24

  1. A posterior over break location across the comms path — device, RTAC, switch, site router, modem, carrier, WAN, firewall, poller, front-end. A distribution, not a guess.
  2. A recommended next action with expected cost: run a probe, remote-reboot, wait, or roll a truck — weighted by that site's drive time and downtime cost.
  3. A value-of-information table ranking what to probe or instrument next, in expected outage minutes avoided.

Sprint rules

  • Ten working days, not fourteen. The last four are write-up and slack.
  • The uncertain part goes in days 1–3, while you can still change plan.
  • Every inference routine gets checked against an exact answer computed another way.
  • Derivation is part of done. Some sprints spend four days on a proof that produces no code. Those are the ones that pay off in the exam.
  • Five-minute demo, or it won't survive a busy month.

Standing references

Sources that apply across the whole track rather than to a single sprint.

Closest prior art — read these early

  • Steinder & Sethi, “A survey of fault localization techniques in computer networks,” Science of Computer Programming 53(2), 2004. The canonical survey of exactly your problem. Start here.
  • Bahl et al., “Towards Highly Reliable Enterprise Network Services via Inference of Multi-Level Dependencies” (Sherlock), SIGCOMM 2007. Bayesian inference over a dependency graph to localize faults — the paper closest to what you're building.
  • Kandula, Mahajan et al., “Detailed Diagnosis in Enterprise Networks,” SIGCOMM 2009.

Alarm management standards

  • EEMUA 191, Alarm Systems: A Guide to Design, Management and Procurement. The operator-load numbers that justify the whole project.
  • ISA-18.2 / IEC 62682, Management of Alarm Systems for the Process Industries.
  • Izadi et al., “An introduction to alarm analysis and design,” IFAC. Good entry to the alarm-flood literature.

Books worth buying that you don't own

  • Darwiche, Modeling and Reasoning with Bayesian Networks (2009). Clearer than Koller on inference algorithms specifically; the better desk reference for Sprints 3–5.
  • Puterman, Markov Decision Processes (1994). The rigorous treatment behind Sutton & Barto ch. 4.
  • Wasserman, All of Statistics. Compact and fast; fills gaps in Sprint 2 better than a full stats text.
  • Murphy, Probabilistic Machine Learning: Advanced Topics (2023). Free PDF from the author. Current coverage of variational methods.

Platform track — run in parallel, not as sprints

RTAC and Ignition are procedural competence, not derivation work, and mixing them into the sprint cadence breaks both. Run them continuously underneath at a few hours a week. Two places they touch the sprints directly:

  • Sprint 1 should emit records shaped like your real stack — RTAC event log format, Ignition tag-change and alarm-journal rows, actual quality codes and timestamp formats. Generic events mean swapping in real logs later is a rewrite.
  • Sprint 3's observation model is exactly your platforms' semantics: what Ignition tag quality means, how RTAC surfaces poll failure, how a DNP3 timeout differs from a Modbus one. Get these wrong and the math can't save the inference.

On the reading load

Koller & Friedman is 1,280 pages and you are not reading it cover to cover. Read the chapters named in each sprint and move on. Same for Bishop. The papers are short and mostly more useful than the textbook chapters for implementation detail — Rabiner in particular will get you further on Sprint 6 than Bishop ch. 13 will.
